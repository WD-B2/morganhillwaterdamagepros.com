What a Sudden Jump in Your Water Bill Can Mean in Morgan Hill, CA

A higher bill without a reason often points to a leak. A simple meter test can tell you whether water is moving when it should not be.

Call (844) 950-6900

By the Morgan Hill Water Damage Pros team | Published November 12, 2026.

A sudden jump in a water bill usually means more water is passing the meter. Common causes are a running toilet, a leaking irrigation line, a dripping fixture or a hidden leak in a pipe, including one under a slab. To test, turn off every tap and appliance, read the meter, wait an hour and read it again. If the number moved, water is leaking somewhere.

Some increases have ordinary causes, such as guests, a new pool fill or a change in billing period. The checks below help you separate those from a real leak.

A ten-minute meter test

If the meter stays still, the leak, if any, may be on a different day or the increase may be use-related.

  1. Shut off water use. Turn off taps, dishwashers, washers and ice makers.
  2. Find the meter. It is usually in a box near the curb.
  3. Read the dial or leak indicator. Many meters have a small triangle or dial that spins with any flow.
  4. Wait an hour without using water. Then read again.
  5. Compare. Any movement means a leak between the meter and your fixtures.

Likely causes in order of frequency

Start with the easy ones. A toilet flapper that does not seal can run for days. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait without flushing and check the bowl.

When the leak is hidden

If the meter keeps moving and you cannot see water, a pipe may be leaking in a wall, under a floor or under the slab. Hot spots on floors, a sound of running water and damp carpet are clues. Our slab leak water damage page explains that case.

A plumber with leak detection tools can find the break. Once the leak is fixed, damp materials need drying, and our structural drying crews handle that.

Billing explanations

A bill may jump because of a longer billing period, a rate change, an estimated read or seasonal use. Compare the usage in units, not the dollar amount, against the same month last year.

Ask your water provider to check the meter if the reading seems wrong. Some utilities offer leak adjustments, and policies vary. According to most utility guidelines, proof such as a plumber invoice is required, so ask what yours accepts. Ask what yours offers and what proof it needs.

What to do if you find a leak

  • Shut off the main valve if water is escaping inside the house.
  • Photograph any damage and the meter reading.
  • Call a plumber for repair and a crew for drying if materials are wet.
  • Tell your insurer about the damage, even if you are unsure about coverage.
  • Keep receipts and records for any adjustment request.

Irrigation and outdoor leaks

Outdoor leaks are easy to miss. A sprinkler line with a cracked head can run for weeks beneath the lawn. Look for soggy spots, a sudden green patch or pooled water near the curb.

Run each zone and walk the yard. Look for geysers, mist and puddles. A leaking valve in a box can hiss quietly and waste a lot of water.

If you have a pool, check the fill line and the auto-fill valve. A stuck valve can add large amounts of water to a bill without any visible sign.

Water heaters, softeners and appliances

A water heater relief valve that drips can add to a bill and signal a pressure problem. A water softener that regenerates too often can also use more water than you expect.

Appliances such as ice makers, dishwashers and washing machines can leak slowly behind or under the unit. Pull the unit out and look at the hose connections.

Replace worn hoses and valves before they fail. Our appliance leak water damage page explains what happens when they do.

Using last year's bill as a benchmark

Pull up your bill for the same month last year and compare usage in units. Weather, guests and watering schedules change use, so month-to-month comparisons can mislead.

A jump of a small percentage may be normal. A jump of double or more without a reason deserves a meter test. Keep a record of your readings over a few weeks to see the trend.

If you see a steady climb, check the toilets again, since a flapper can degrade over time. If use spikes on certain days, think about what changed, such as a new irrigation schedule.

What this means in Morgan Hill

The City of Morgan Hill's water comes from groundwater wells in the Coyote and Llagas aquifers, and its 2021 report lists hardness near 302 mg/L. Scale builds up on faucet aerators and toilet fill valves, which can cause leaks and slow flow. Cleaning or replacing small parts is a cheap early step.

Homes in unincorporated places such as San Martin may be on wells or small water systems with different billing, so check with your supplier. For properties near Coyote, also check outdoor lines, since long irrigation runs can leak unseen.
