Water Damage and Selling a House in Morgan Hill, CA: Disclosure Basics

California sellers must disclose known material defects. Past water damage that was fixed properly is easier to explain when you have records.

Call (844) 950-6900

By the Morgan Hill Water Damage Pros team | Published November 12, 2026.

California sellers of one-to-four-unit homes generally must give buyers a Transfer Disclosure Statement and disclose known material defects. Past water damage, and whether it was repaired, is the kind of fact buyers care about. Keep records of the cause, the repair and the drying, and ask your agent or a real estate attorney what to put on the forms.

This is general information, not legal advice. The rules turn on the facts, and a licensed professional should advise you on your sale.

What sellers usually disclose

The Transfer Disclosure Statement covers items such as structural problems, damage, a leaky roof, appliances and whether they work. Sellers must disclose known defects that are material, meaning important to a buyer's decision. A seller does not have a duty to search for hidden problems. This follows the guidelines of California Civil Code section 1102, which governs the Transfer Disclosure Statement.

The Natural Hazard Disclosure also asks whether the property is in a special flood hazard area. That is separate from past water damage but relates to water risk.

Records that help

Buyers and their inspectors respond well to documentation. A repaired leak with a paper trail reads very differently from an unexplained stain.

Before you list: fix the source and dry the structure

An unresolved leak will show up at inspection. Fix the source, dry the structure and repair finishes before showing the home. Our structural drying crews provide moisture readings that document the drying.

If mold was present, a written record of remediation and any independent clearance helps. Our mold remediation page explains the work, though we do not issue clearance reports.

Buyers' inspections and negotiation

Inspectors use moisture meters and thermal cameras. A reading above normal can lead to a re-inspection request or a credit demand. A seller with records can answer questions with facts.

Be honest and specific. Describing what happened, what was done and when is better than a vague answer, and it reduces the chance of a later dispute.

Ask your agent or attorney about

  • Exactly how to answer each water-related question on the disclosure forms.
  • Whether past insurance claims need to appear anywhere.
  • How long ago repairs must be described.
  • Whether a pre-listing inspection makes sense.
  • What to do if the buyer finds an issue after closing.

Timing: repair first or sell as is

Some owners repair fully before listing, and others sell as is with a credit. Repairing first lets you present the home well and control the story. Selling as is saves time but may invite lower offers and tough negotiations.

If you sell as is, disclose what you know and provide records. Buyers who feel informed negotiate more calmly than those who feel surprised.

Ask your agent how local buyers have reacted to similar situations. Their experience is worth more than guesses.

Working with the buyer's inspection

Expect inspectors to check ceilings, under sinks, around the water heater and in the crawl space or attic. Make those areas easy to reach. Clear storage from under sinks and open access hatches.

If an inspector flags moisture, ask for the reading and location in writing. A follow-up check by a restoration company can give a clear answer on whether it is active.

Respond to requests with facts and receipts. Keep the tone calm, since most disputes grow from misunderstanding, not from the water itself.

What not to do

Selling is easier when the story is clear. A repaired, documented leak is just a line in the history of the home.

  • Do not paint over stains without fixing the source.
  • Do not hide a known defect from buyers or their agents.
  • Do not skip records, since receipts and logs make explanations simple.
  • Do not guess on disclosure forms when you can ask your agent.
  • Do not argue with an inspector, since a calm response gets better results.

What this means in Morgan Hill

About one-twelfth of Morgan Hill is in a FEMA high-risk flood zone, so buyers here often ask about flood history and flood insurance. Know your parcel's status before you list, and keep any flood claims paperwork together.

If your sale involves property near Coyote or rural land near San Martin, also gather records on wells, septic systems and drainage. Those items matter to buyers in those areas.
